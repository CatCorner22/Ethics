# Psychedelic and related medicines: recommended best practices by drug

**Not legal or medical advice.** This is a research synthesis for discussion by licensed professionals. It is not a clinical guideline, not a regulatory opinion, and not authorization to possess, prescribe, or administer controlled substances. Law, labels, and trial results in this field change quickly. Anything marked **[verify]** was written from memory of the literature and regulator materials and **was not re-checked against live sources** when this file was drafted (November 2026). Check the current FDA label, DEA/Health Canada notices, state or provincial law, and your own college or board before acting.

Companion files: [process-level best practices (recording, cooling-off, chaperones, consent timing, etc.)](psychedelic-best-practices-process.md) and the [24-concept ethics framework](psychedelic-ethics-research-chat.md) (Track A: monitored dosing without psychotherapy; Track B: psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy).

## How to read the evidence tags

| Tag | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Approved** | A regulator has approved the product for the stated indication (label governs). |
| **Trial-grade** | Randomized trial data exist but no approval for this indication; practice should be research or an exceptional-access pathway. |
| **Consensus / expert opinion** | Protocols come from trial manuals, regulator documents, or expert panels, not from comparative trials. |
| **Anecdotal / unregulated** | Mostly observational, case reports, or retreat experience. |

Common limits across the field: small samples, **functional unblinding** (participants usually know whether they got the drug), high expectancy effects, short follow-up, under-reporting of harms, and trial populations that exclude many real-world patients (Muthukumaraswamy et al. 2021; Aday et al. 2020; CANMAT 2023; FDA PDAC briefing, 4 June 2024).

## Cross-cutting floor (applies to every drug below)

These come from the human-hallucinogen safety guidelines and later consensus work, and apply unless a drug section says otherwise.

- **Screening:** psychiatric history (psychosis spectrum, bipolar I, first-degree family history of either, active suicidality, severe personality disorder, substance use), cardiovascular and hepatic status, pregnancy/lactation, full medication and supplement review (serotonergic agents, MAOIs, lithium, QT-prolonging drugs, CYP2D6/3A4 interactions). Johnson, Richards & Griffiths 2008 remains the usual starting point; trial manuals (Compass, Usona, MAPS/Lykos) add drug-specific exclusions.
- **Setting:** quiet, private, medically equipped room; ability to reach emergency care; no sharp objects/unsafe exits; restroom access; two trained people for hours-long sessions where feasible (Health Canada trial risk notices; FDA PDAC 2024).
- **Monitoring:** continuous presence of a trained person; periodic blood pressure/heart rate; escalation criteria written in advance; a clinician able to treat medical emergencies reachable.
- **Preparation / integration:** at least one preparation contact and a post-session follow-up within days (details in the companion file). Track A (no psychotherapy) should say so and refer for integration rather than improvising therapy.
- **Discharge:** released only when oriented and steady; no driving or high-stakes decisions that day (longer for some drugs); accompanied transport.
- **Adverse events:** record, treat, report to the regulator or program where required, and give patients the college/board complaint route (see companion file).
- **Diversion:** no take-home supply of Schedule I-type psychedelics outside a sanctioned program; locked storage, counts, and reconciliation for controlled substances; pharmacist as independent checkpoint (OCP/APhA codes; TGA does not anticipate take-home for MDMA/psilocybin).

## 1. Psilocybin

**Regulatory status.** US: Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act; FDA breakthrough-therapy designations for Compass Pathways (COMP360, treatment-resistant depression, 2018) and Usona (major depressive disorder, 2019). State programs: Oregon (Measure 109, supervised adult-use "psilocybin services" since 2023), Colorado (Proposition 122, natural medicine healing centers phased in from 2025), New Mexico (Medical Psilocybin Act, 2025, program build-out scheduled for later years) **[verify]**. These state programs are **not FDA approval** and not medical prescribing; federal law still applies. Canada: psilocybin is Schedule III under the CDSA; Health Canada's SAP resumed granting case-by-case access to psilocybin and MDMA for practitioners in January 2022; clinical trials and s.56 exemptions are other routes. Alberta regulates psychedelic drug treatment services under its 2023 standards.

**Evidence.** Phase 2 trials in depression (Carhart-Harris 2021 vs escitalopram; Davis 2021 *JAMA Psychiatry*; Goodwin 2022 *NEJM* dose-finding for COMP360 in TRD) and alcohol use disorder (Bogenschutz 2022 *JAMA Psychiatry*). Phase 3 programs reported in 2025 **[verify]**. CANMAT 2023 graded the evidence low and the treatment experimental. Common caveats: unblinding, expectancy, small samples, few diverse participants.

**Screening and contraindications.** Personal or first-degree family history of psychosis or bipolar I; unstable cardiovascular disease or uncontrolled hypertension; pregnancy; lithium (reports of seizures/ unpredictable reactions in observational data); active mania; severe untreated suicidality in many protocols. Serotonergic antidepressants may blunt effects and are usually tapered only under a prescriber's plan—do not stop psychotropics casually (FDA PDAC concerns; Alberta s.5).

**Dosing and setting.** Trial doses are typically fixed oral doses in the ~10–25 mg (psilocybin) range for Compass-style protocols and weight- or flat-dose regimens in other trials; Oregon uses licensed facilitators in a licensed service center without medical prescribing. Session length is typically 6–8 hours. Eyeshades and music are common in trials. Do not copy trial doses outside a protocol; follow the product, program, or protocol you are authorized under.

**Monitoring.** Continuous presence; blood pressure and heart rate at intervals (transient rises are common); watch for severe anxiety, panic, paranoia, disorientation, and vomiting; clear rescue plan (reassurance first, medication only per prescriber protocol, e.g. benzodiazepine for severe agitation in some trials).

**Preparation.** One to three contacts covering expectations, intentions, touch/recording policy, what challenging experiences can look like, and who to call. Avoid promising mystical or "breakthrough" outcomes.

**Integration.** Follow-up within about 24–72 hours and again within weeks; psychotherapy integration in Track B, referral in Track A. Watch for delayed distress, derealization, mania, and relationship disruption.

**Adverse events.** Transient anxiety, nausea, headache, elevated BP/HR; less commonly persistent perceptual disturbance (HPPD), prolonged psychological distress, psychosis or mania in vulnerable people; suicidal ideation can occur in trials and has been reported at follow-up (Schlag et al. 2022; Breeksema et al. 2020 on challenging experiences). Sexual boundary violations are a documented risk category in psychedelic therapy more broadly.

**Abuse / diversion.** Lower classical abuse liability than stimulants or opioids, but Schedule I status means strict custody; Oregon/Colorado have track-and-trace requirements for products.

**Key sources.** Johnson, Richards & Griffiths 2008, *J Psychopharmacol* 22:603; Carhart-Harris et al. 2021 *NEJM*; Davis et al. 2021 *JAMA Psychiatry*; Goodwin et al. 2022 *NEJM*; Bogenschutz et al. 2022 *JAMA Psychiatry*; Schlag et al. 2022 *J Psychopharmacol*; CANMAT 2023 (*Can J Psychiatry*); Oregon Health Authority psilocybin services rules; Colorado Natural Medicine Division rules; Health Canada SAP notices; Alberta Psychedelic Drug Treatment Services Standards (2023).

## 2. MDMA

**Regulatory status.** US: Schedule I. On 4 June 2024 the FDA Psychopharmacologic Drugs Advisory Committee voted 9–2 that efficacy was not shown and 10–1 that benefits did not outweigh risks for midomafetamine-assisted therapy for PTSD; the FDA issued a complete response letter in August 2024 asking for another trial **[verify exact date]**. Canada: Schedule I CDSA; SAP access since 2022; trials. Australia (TGA) has permitted psychiatrist-only authorised prescribing since 1 July 2023 for PTSD (see framework file).

**Evidence.** Two phase 3 trials (Mitchell 2021 *Nat Med*; Mitchell 2023 *Nat Med*) showed large PTSD improvements versus placebo with therapy. Critics (FDA advisers, ICER 2024, others) cited functional unblinding, expectancy, therapist-effect confounding, incomplete cardiac data, missing abuse-potential and adverse-event capture, and documented sexual misconduct in phase 2.

**Screening and contraindications.** Uncontrolled hypertension or significant cardiovascular disease; arrhythmia or QT prolongation; hepatic impairment; pregnancy; history of ventricular arrhythmia; psychosis or bipolar I (trial exclusions); current SSRI/SNRI/MAOI use requiring washout under supervision (serotonin syndrome risk with MAOIs is serious); active substance use disorder as defined by the protocol. Hyponatremia risk: advise against drinking large amounts of water.

**Dosing and setting.** Trial manuals used an initial 80–120 mg dose with an optional supplemental half dose about 1.5–2 hours later, in sessions lasting roughly 8 hours with two therapists, plus several preparation and integration sessions. Use only under a protocol or program; no take-home use.

**Monitoring.** Continuous observation by two trained persons; frequent BP/HR and temperature checks; hydration without overhydration; watch for hyperthermia, hyponatremia, arrhythmia, severe anxiety, and impaired judgment; overnight stay or accompanied discharge per protocol (many protocols included an overnight stay).

**Preparation / integration.** Trauma-focused therapy frame needs a named method (Track B) and trauma-informed boundaries. Integration sessions within about 24 hours and in the days after; ongoing suicide risk screening (Columbia scale commonly used). In the next days patients may report a "low mood" period; monitor.

**Adverse events.** Elevated BP/HR/temperature, jaw clenching, nausea, fatigue, low mood days after, anxiety, insomnia; rare hyperthermia, hyponatremia, arrhythmia; documented therapist boundary violations in trial settings (FDA PDAC; *Power Trip* journalism; independent reviews).

**Abuse / diversion.** Recreational use is widespread; adulterated street "MDMA" is a harm-reduction issue. Programs need secured storage and dose accountability; the FDA discussed REMS elements including external safety reporting.

**Key sources.** Mitchell et al. 2021 and 2023 *Nat Med*; FDA PDAC briefing and transcript (4 June 2024); ICER evidence report on MDMA for PTSD (2024); Health Canada SAP and trial notices; MAPS Code of Ethics for Psychedelic Psychotherapy v4.

## 3. Ketamine (racemic) and esketamine (Spravato)

**Regulatory status.** US: ketamine is Schedule III, approved as an anesthetic; depression use is **off-label**. Esketamine nasal spray (Spravato) was approved by FDA in March 2019 for treatment-resistant depression with a REMS, and later for depressive symptoms in MDD with acute suicidal ideation; an approval as monotherapy followed in 2025 **[verify]**. The FDA warned in October 2023 about compounded ketamine products. Telehealth prescribing of controlled substances has relied on repeated DEA flexibilities **[verify current expiry]**. Canada: ketamine is a CDSA-controlled, prescription-only drug; esketamine is approved for TRD (Health Canada, 2020 **[verify]**). College guidance: CPSBC ketamine professional guideline (IV only in hospital/accredited facility; monitor sedation; diversion expectations), CPSO has no ketamine-specific policy but CAM policy and premises (OHPIP) apply; KATA/OMA Psychedelic MIG recommendations (2023).

**Evidence.** Strongest rapid-antidepressant evidence among these drugs: Berman 2000; Zarate 2006; Murrough 2013; Popova 2019 *Am J Psychiatry* (esketamine plus oral antidepressant); ESCAPE-TRD (Reif 2023 *NEJM*, esketamine vs quetiapine XR). Effects are often short-lived without maintenance; long-term safety data are limited; Sanacora et al. 2017 *JAMA Psychiatry* consensus statement is the key US expert document.

**Screening and contraindications.** Esketamine label: aneurysmal vascular disease, arteriovenous malformation, history of intracerebral hemorrhage, hypersensitivity. Use caution/exclude: uncontrolled hypertension or cardiovascular instability, active psychosis, active substance use disorder (especially stimulants/alcohol), pregnancy, severe hepatic impairment, raised intracranial pressure; bladder symptoms (repeated high-dose use is linked to cystitis). Baseline BP, medication review (sedatives/opioids add respiratory depression risk).

**Dosing and setting.** Spravato: 56 or 84 mg intranasal; twice weekly for four weeks, then weekly/biweekly, with **supervised self-administration** and at least **2 hours of post-dose observation**; BP checked before and about 40 minutes after dose; patient may not drive until the next day after a restful sleep. IV racemic ketamine: typically 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes in clinics, with anesthesia-capable monitoring; IM/SC/oral/sublingual practice varies and has less standardised evidence.

**Monitoring.** Blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation (IV/IM), sedation level, dissociation, nausea; extended observation for sedation after the end of dissociation (CPSBC emphasises post-dose sedation, not only vitals); a clinician able to manage airway and hypertensive/dissociative emergencies.

**Preparation / integration.** Role split recommended: mental-health professional (intake, prep, integration), medical professional (physical safety), patient; one person may fill both roles only if qualified in both. Make clear whether the service is monitored dosing (Track A) or psychotherapy-enhanced (Track B).

**Adverse events.** Dissociation, transient BP rise, nausea, dizziness, sedation, anxiety; urinary tract toxicity and liver enzyme elevation with chronic use; abuse/misuse; deaths after non-clinical use or in unmonitored/compounded settings (the 2023 death of actor Matthew Perry, with later criminal charges against suppliers, drew attention to diversion).

**Abuse / diversion.** Real: high street value, "K-hole" recreational use, compounded take-home products. Best practice: in-clinic administration for Schedule III products where possible, pill/vial counts, locked storage, documented chain of custody, prescription monitoring program checks (US), and no bulk prescribing to patients with misuse history without a plan. Spravato REMS bars take-home use.

**Key sources.** FDA Spravato prescribing information and REMS; Sanacora et al. 2017; Popova 2019; Reif 2023; CPSBC ketamine guideline v1.4; KATA & OMA Psychedelic MIG (2023); FDA 2023 compounded ketamine alert; *Ethical Guidelines for Ketamine Clinicians* (*J Psychedelic Psychiatry*).

## 4. LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide)

**Regulatory status.** US Schedule I; Canada Schedule III. Not approved anywhere for a medical indication. Industry-sponsored development (for example MindMed's MM120 for generalized anxiety disorder) is in phase 2b/3 **[verify current stage]**; academic Swiss compassionate-use LSD-assisted therapy has existed since 2014.

**Evidence.** Gasser et al. 2014/2015 pilot for anxiety in life-threatening illness; Holze et al. 2021 and later phase 2 work; a phase 2b dose-ranging trial in GAD published in 2025 **[verify citation]**. Evidence base is small and early.

**Screening and contraindications.** As for classic psychedelics: psychosis spectrum/bipolar I (personal or first-degree family), pregnancy, uncontrolled cardiovascular disease; avoid with lithium; review serotonergic and antipsychotic medications. Duration is long (about 8–12 hours), so physical stamina and cardiovascular stability matter.

**Dosing and setting.** Trials use microgram doses (roughly 25–200 µg ranges); sessions are long, so staffing and discharge planning must cover 10–12 hours of effect plus a longer tail.

**Monitoring.** Same as psilocybin but over longer time: BP/HR, agitation, panic, prolonged confusion, and sleep disruption afterward.

**Preparation / integration.** Track B protocols emphasise preparation and integration; Track A should refer integration out.

**Adverse events.** Anxiety, headache, nausea, transient BP rise; HPPD; psychosis in predisposed people; "bad trips" with distress; sleep disturbance.

**Abuse / diversion.** Low dependence liability; high Schedule I diversion and adulteration issues in the illicit market (NBOMe/other substitutes sold as LSD are dangerous).

**Key sources.** Holze et al. (Basel studies); Gasser et al.; Johnson et al. 2008; Schlag et al. 2022.

## 5. Ayahuasca and DMT

**Regulatory status.** US: DMT is Schedule I; ayahuasca (DMT plus an MAOI) is treated as containing a controlled substance. *Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita Beneficente União do Vegetal*, 546 U.S. 418 (2006) required the government to justify burdening sincere religious use under RFRA; the Santo Daime-affiliated *Church of the Holy Light of the Queen* also obtained an Oregon federal ruling (2009). These exemptions cover **religious practice**, not medical practice. Canada: DMT is Schedule III; Health Canada s.56 exemptions (since 2017) for UDV and Santo Daime members. Some US cities decriminalize "entheogenic plants"; Colorado's natural medicine program covers DMT (non-peyote plant medicines) with a later start than psilocybin **[verify]**.

**Evidence.** Small randomized trial in treatment-resistant depression (Palhano-Fontes 2019 *Psychol Med*); observational and ceremonial data; Gable 2007 risk assessment; synthetic DMT trials (for example Small Pharma SPL026 phase 2a) are early **[verify]**.

**Screening and contraindications.** MAOI interaction danger: serotonergic antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, dextromethorphan, stimulants, MDMA, some cold medicines) can cause serotonin syndrome or hypertensive crisis; tyramine-rich foods; psychosis spectrum/bipolar I; significant cardiovascular disease; pregnancy; pheochromocytoma and other conditions exacerbated by MAOIs. Dietary restrictions before ceremonies are traditional but should be medically sensible.

**Dosing and setting.** Variable brew strength; oral duration about 4–6 hours; vomiting/diarrhea ("purge") is common and carries dehydration and aspiration risks. Because brew potency is not standardized, medical settings require a standardised, analytically verified product.

**Monitoring.** BP/HR, temperature, hydration, airway protection during vomiting, agitation/psychosis screening; trained person able to recognise serotonin syndrome and hypertensive emergency.

**Preparation / integration.** Cultural respect: do not claim ceremonial authority you lack; honor Indigenous and religious frameworks without appropriation (Celidwen et al. 2023 "eight Rs"). Medical settings should not imitate a church or blur frames.

**Adverse events.** Vomiting, diarrhea, anxiety, transient hypertension; deaths have been reported in unregulated retreats (adverse interactions, cardiac events, trauma, and sexual assault allegations in some settings).

**Abuse / diversion.** Low dependence; the greater risk is unsafe supply and unregulated "retreat" operators selling clinical claims. DMT as a vaporized/injected synthetic is a separate product category with short duration.

**Key sources.** *Gonzales v. UDV* (2006); Health Canada s.56 exemption notices; Palhano-Fontes et al. 2019; Gable 2007 *Addiction*; Celidwen et al. 2023.

## 6. 5-MeO-DMT (including toad-derived) and other short-acting tryptamines

**Regulatory status.** US Schedule I; Canada Schedule III **[verify]**. Industry programs (for example GH Research's GH001 inhaled 5-MeO-DMT) are early-phase **[verify]**.

**Best-practice notes.** Very short, intense onset (minutes). Medical readiness for sudden loss of control, vomiting, hypertension, and (rarely) arrhythmia. Contraindicated with MAOIs. Toad (*Incilius alvarius*) sourcing raises **ecological** and cultural concerns; prefer synthetic. Cardiac and psychosis screening as for other classic psychedelics. Evidence is largely survey-based and early open-label.

**Key sources.** Reckweg et al. 2022 and 2023 on 5-MeO-DMT in early trials **[verify]**; Celidwen 2023 on sourcing ethics.

## 7. Ibogaine / noribogaine

**Regulatory status.** US Schedule I; no federal approval. In 2025 Texas appropriated public funds to support ibogaine clinical research (matching-fund structure) and other states (for example Arizona) funded studies **[verify amounts]**. Canada: ibogaine is not a CDSA-scheduled substance but is a prescription drug under the food and drug regulations **[verify]**. Most treatment occurs in clinics in Mexico and elsewhere, outside US/Canadian regulation.

**Evidence.** Mostly observational; a small open-label study in veterans with traumatic brain injury (Cherian et al. 2024 *Nat Med*) used magnesium co-administration and cardiac monitoring and reported improvements in disability/psychiatric measures; opioid-withdrawal data are mostly retrospective (Noller 2018; Brown & Alper 2018).

**Screening and contraindications.** **Cardiac risk is the central issue**: QT prolongation, torsades de pointes, and sudden death (hERG blockade; Alper 2012 case series; Koenig & Hilber 2015). Exclude or manage with specialist input: baseline long QT, bradycardia, structural heart disease, electrolyte abnormalities (potassium, magnesium), liver impairment, concomitant QT-prolonging drugs, strong CYP2D6 inhibitors; also screen for psychosis and seizure disorders. Microdosing does not waive cardiac screening (Brunt 2026 *Addiction* per the framework file).

**Dosing and setting.** Dose is not standardized across providers; cardiac-capable setting with continuous ECG, defibrillation capability, IV access, and a clinician trained to treat torsades; correct potassium/magnesium before dosing; avoid dosing patients on opioids or sedatives without medically supervised withdrawal planning.

**Monitoring.** Continuous ECG with QTc tracking for the entire acute period (about 24–36 hours of effects and a longer elimination tail), electrolytes, vitals, ataxia and fall prevention, nausea/vomiting management.

**Preparation / integration.** Opioid-dependent patients are at **overdose risk after treatment** because tolerance falls; arrange relapse-prevention, naloxone, and continuing addiction care. Integration support is essential; do not present ibogaine as a "cure."

**Adverse events.** QT prolongation, torsades, cardiac arrest, seizures, ataxia, nausea, prolonged visual effects; deaths have occurred in supervised settings.

**Abuse / diversion.** Primary risks are unsafe supply and unsupervised use; no take-home.

**Key sources.** Alper et al. 2012; Koenig & Hilber 2015 *Molecules*; Cherian et al. 2024 *Nat Med*; Noller et al. 2018 *Am J Drug Alcohol Abuse*; Brown & Alper 2018; Brunt 2026.

## 8. Mescaline (peyote, San Pedro, synthetic)

**Regulatory status.** US: mescaline and peyote are Schedule I, but federal regulations exempt bona fide **Native American Church** use of peyote (21 CFR 1307.31; AIRFA amendments 1994). The exemption is religious and tied to NAC membership, not a medical authorization. State bans on peyote cultivation or use may still apply. Canada: mescaline (and peyote) are Schedule III, with no general NAC exemption comparable to the US rule **[verify]**. Colorado's natural medicine framework includes mescaline derived from non-peyote sources, not peyote **[verify]**.

**Evidence.** Very limited: historical studies and small modern analyses; early-phase synthetic mescaline research exists but is not mature **[verify]**.

**Screening, dosing, monitoring.** Treat as other long-acting classic psychedelics (10–12 hour effect): screen for psychosis spectrum and cardiovascular disease, avoid MAOI combinations where relevant, monitor BP/HR and nausea/vomiting (common). No standard medical dose exists.

**Ethics specific to mescaline.** Peyote is ecologically threatened and culturally central to Native communities; non-Native clinics should prefer synthetic or cultivated alternatives (San Pedro) and avoid extracting peyote (Celidwen 2023 reverence, reparation; NAC advocacy statements). Do not blur the NAC sacramental frame with medical practice.

**Adverse events.** Nausea, vomiting, anxiety, transient BP/HR rise; psychosis in predisposed people.

**Key sources.** 21 CFR 1307.31; AIRFA amendments (1994); ABA Health Law religious-use article (2025); Celidwen et al. 2023.

| Agent | Notes |

| **Nitrous oxide** | Small trials in TRD (for example Nagele et al. 2015 *Biol Psychiatry*, Nagele 2021 *Biol Psychiatry* **[verify]**). Not a controlled substance; occupational/misuse hazards (B12 deficiency, neuropathy) and scavenging requirements. |

## Cross-drug comparison of safety floors

| Concern | Classic (psilocybin, LSD, mescaline) | MDMA | Ketamine/esketamine | Ayahuasca/DMT | Ibogaine |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main medical emergency | Severe agitation, hypertensive spike | Hyperthermia, hyponatremia, arrhythmia | Respiratory depression (esp. with sedatives), BP spike | Serotonin syndrome, hypertensive crisis, aspiration | QT prolongation / torsades, cardiac arrest |
| Staffing floor (consensus) | Trained observer(s), clinician reachable | Two trained persons, medical backup | Clinician with airway/BP skills; REMS for Spravato | Medically competent staff, drug-interaction screening | Cardiac-capable team with continuous ECG |
| Take-home | Not appropriate | Not appropriate | Spravato: not allowed; compounded ketamine: contested | Not appropriate | Not appropriate |
| Special regulator concern | Hype, unblinding | Misconduct, cardiac data gaps | Diversion, compounding | Religious vs medical frame | Cardiac deaths, opioid relapse overdose |
