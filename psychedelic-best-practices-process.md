# Process-level best practices for patient and provider outcomes

**Not legal or medical advice.** Research synthesis for discussion by licensed professionals. Items marked **[verify]** were written from memory and not re-checked against live sources (drafted November 2026). Laws and college standards differ by jurisdiction and change; confirm with your regulator and counsel.

Companion files: [per-drug best practices](psychedelic-best-practices-by-drug.md) and the [24-concept framework (Track A / Track B)](psychedelic-ethics-research-chat.md).

## Evidence honesty up front

Almost none of the practices below has been tested in a randomized trial of **practice-level** outcomes (for example, "recording reduces misconduct"). Support comes from (a) psychedelic-specific consensus papers and regulator standards, (b) analogies from medicine, psychotherapy, and consumer protection, and (c) documented failures, such as sexual misconduct in a phase 2 MDMA trial discussed at the FDA advisory committee on 4 June 2024. Treat "evidence" below as **strength of rationale**, not proof of effect. Each section ends with criticisms.

| Practice | Rationale strength | Direct psychedelic evidence of effect | Main trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Informed consent in stages | Strong (general medical ethics) | Qualitative/consensus only | Preparation can prime expectations |
| Recording of active sessions | Moderate (deterrence, review) | None on outcomes | Privacy, subpoena, chilling effects |
| Two-person dosing teams / chaperone | Moderate to strong (AMA, FSMB) | Regulator/trial standards, no outcome trials | Cost, patient comfort, collusion |
| Touch consent protocol | Moderate | Consensus (JAMA 2024; McHerron 2025) | Rigid rules vs relational care |
| Cooling-off / no-sales period | Moderate by analogy | None | Delay, access, paternalism |
| Clinical supervision/consultation | Strong in psychotherapy | Indirect | Cost, availability of qualified supervisors |
| Structured follow-up | Strong (safety) | Trial protocols | Resource use |
| Independent complaint routes | Strong (professional regulation) | Indirect | Fear of retaliation; slow processes |
| Provider wellbeing supports | Moderate (burnout literature) | Almost none | Hard to separate from marketing |

## 1. Informed consent: content and timing

**Recommended practice.**

1. **Stage it.** Give written information early (at screening or booking), discuss it in a preparation visit, and **reconfirm** on dosing day before ingestion while the patient is sober and has capacity. Do not obtain first-time consent on the dosing day under time or payment pressure. Where a payment is due, the consent conversation should come first and be separable from the sale.
2. **Content.** Indication and evidence grade; investigational/off-label/SAP status; alternatives including conventional care and no treatment; conflicts of interest and fees; expected time course; physical and psychiatric risks; possible lasting changes in values, beliefs, or relationships (enhanced consent; Smith & Sisti 2020; Jacobs 2023); possibility of no benefit; suggestibility; who will be in the room; **recording policy; touch policy; limits of confidentiality, including that insurers, employers, workers' compensation boards, or courts may compel records** (College of Alberta Psychologists 2025).
3. **Capacity rule.** Bind optional preferences before dosing; after ingestion allow patients to tighten but not loosen restrictions (touch, visitors, recording). Intoxicated speech is not later sexual or financial consent (see the framework file).
4. **Process, not a form.** Teach-back, time for questions, a copy of the document, and an explicit right to withdraw before and during the session.

**Evidence.** General informed-consent law and AMA Opinion 2.1.1 (2024) support documented, capacitous consent. Psychedelic-specific papers (HOPE 2024; Neuroethics 2026 consent study; Brittain et al. 2025 scoping review) recommend consent as a process, including disclosure of disappointment and identity-level change. Oregon requires pre-session preparation and written client information; Colorado requires consent each session **[verify specifics]**.

**Criticisms.** (a) The transformative-experience critique: patients cannot fully know the experience beforehand, so "understanding" is limited (Paul; Jacobs 2023); defenders say aspiration to leave a bad status quo can ground consent (Villiger 2024). (b) Preparation both informs and **primes** expectancy, which may inflate effects or amplify nocebo. (c) Long forms can become liability shields rather than communication.

## 2. Session recording

**Recommended practice.** Offer **audio-visual recording of the active dosing phase** as an optional or default safeguard, with: written, separate consent for recording and for storage/access; a stated retention period; role-based access (named reviewers, not marketing); storage as a health record under privacy law; patient right to ask that recording pause for private needs (for example toileting) with the pause logged; explicit statement that recordings are not testimonials or promotional material; and a defined review use (quality review, complaint investigation, supervision with consent). The College of Alberta Psychologists/CPSA guidance recommends recording the active phase with written consent **[verify wording]**; MDMA phase 3 trials used video of sessions (and the FDA discussion treated abuse deterrence and external review as important).

**Evidence.** No trial measures whether recording reduces misconduct. The rationale is deterrence, evidence in complaints, and supervision. Recording was available in the MDMA trial program, yet misconduct in earlier phases was exposed through other channels, which shows recording alone is insufficient.

**Criticisms.** Privacy and dignity (patients may be naked of defenses, crying, disinhibited); chilling effect on honest disclosure; breach and subpoena risk (recordings are discoverable and can be demanded by insurers, courts, or employers); consent may be coerced if treatment is conditional on recording; a recorder can create false assurance if no one reviews footage; recordings of identifiable patients complicate research reuse. Mitigations: patient choice with alternative safeguards (second person), minimum retention, encryption, access logs, and legal review before launch.

## 3. "No more sales" and cooling-off periods

**Concept.** Separate **clinical decision-making** from **commercial commitment**: no soliciting purchases, packages, memberships, donations, investments, or testimonials during the acute window and immediately after, and a **delay between consent-to-treatment information and payment/commitment** (for example 24–72 hours for non-urgent packages), with a refund policy before dosing.

**Evidence.** There is no psychedelic trial of cooling-off periods. Support is by analogy: consumer cooling-off rules (for example the US FTC three-day rule for certain door-to-door sales and similar provincial/state statutes), waiting periods in elective cosmetic and bariatric surgery consent practice in some jurisdictions, and **HOPE (2024)** and APA psychiatry resource-document concerns about financial exploitation and "noetic/financial capture" of suggestible patients. The framework (concepts 4, 8, 24) already proposes anti-capture rules.

**Criticisms.** Delays can disadvantage patients with limited access (time off work, travel to a clinic); waiting periods in other health areas (for example some abortion waiting periods) are criticised as paternalistic and burdensome, so the case here rests on **financial** exploitation risk, not on doubting patients' competence; clinics may rebrand "memberships" to evade the spirit of the rule; exception needed for emergencies and for drugs with narrow protocols. Suggested design: allow payment for the **evaluation** immediately, require a cooling-off interval before prepaying multi-session packages, and prohibit upselling or aftercare sales within a set window after a session (e.g., 7 days).

## 4. Chaperones and two-person teams

**Recommended practice.** Two trained persons for hours-long dosing sessions wherever feasible (Health Canada trial risk notices; FDA PDAC 2024; MAPS code), with at least one a licensed clinician. Where intimate examination or touch is required, offer a chaperone and document who was present (AMA Opinion 1.2.4 on use of chaperones; FSMB 2020). Do not leave the patient alone with a single facilitator for extended periods. Do not use a **family member** or untrained assistant as the "chaperone" for a sensitive procedure. Chaperones need training, a stated reporting duty, and independence from the lead clinician's authority (peer-level reporting channel).

**Evidence.** Chaperone policies are standard in medicine to protect patients and clinicians; evidence of effect on misconduct is limited, and chaperone use may reduce reported incidents but also may be inconsistent. In psychedelic trials, co-therapist pairs are standard, yet documented misconduct occurred in a pair-based program, so team size does not replace oversight.

**Criticisms.** Cost and staffing; patient may feel observed or prefer privacy; chaperones can be bystanders who defer to the lead; two persons also double the number of people who know sensitive disclosures; in small-community practice, finding independent chaperones is difficult.

## 5. Touch consent

**Recommended practice (three tiers).** (1) **Safety touch** (vital signs, preventing falls or self-injury) is covered by the general safety consent. (2) **Supportive touch** (for example hand-holding, a hand on the shoulder) only with **written pre-dose agreement** specifying where, why, who initiates, and stop signals, plus in-the-moment verbal consent each time; the patient may withdraw at any moment and the clinician must stop at once (McGuire et al. 2024 *JAMA Netw Open*; APA psychiatry "double consent"). (3) **Sexual or romantic touch is never permitted** (MAPS; FSMB 2020). Do not expand touch consent after dosing begins (McHerron et al. 2025). Use body-map specificity and train staff to read nonverbal withdrawal cues (Neuroethics 2026 qualitative study). Sexual-boundary cooling-off after treatment should follow the **strictest rule** among team licenses (for example Ontario RHPA five-year client status; Colorado facilitator rule two years; MAPS: never).

**Evidence.** Consensus and qualitative work; no outcomes trials. FDA advisers and journalism documented harm from boundary violations in phase 2 MDMA research.

**Criticisms.** Some clinicians and patients argue rigid no-touch rules deprive distressed patients of comfort that might aid safety; others argue that any therapeutic touch in a suggestible state undermines true consent. Written agreements can slide into "boundary drift" if renegotiated mid-session (McHerron 2025). Cultural differences in touch norms complicate standard scripts.

## 6. Supervision, consultation, and training

**Recommended practice.** Written scope for each team member; named **medical director or most-responsible clinician** for dosing-day decisions (CPSO ICRC ketamine case; RANZCP guidance that the psychiatrist keeps overall responsibility); regular clinical supervision or peer consultation for boundary dilemmas; independent (not solely sponsor-run) training; documented competence in pharmacology, emergency response, consent, boundaries, and (Track B) the named therapy method; new practitioners observe or co-treat under a supervisor who has experience with similar patients (RANZCP). Personal use of psychedelics is **not** a requirement and should not be a credential (McGuire 2024 consensus). Facilitators must not consume the substance while working (Oregon rule).

**Evidence.** Supervision is a cornerstone of psychotherapy ethics; psychedelic-specific evidence is mostly regulator rules and trial manuals. Alberta requires a medical director accountable for services (2023 standards).

**Criticisms.** Supervisor shortage; supervisors may also lack psychedelic expertise; supervision can be box-checking; mandatory supervision raises cost and may concentrate care in wealthier clinics.

## 7. Post-session follow-up and aftercare

**Recommended practice.** Same-day safe discharge (no driving; accompanied transport; no major decisions); **contact within 24 hours**, again within about a week, and at set intervals during the following weeks; structured screening for suicidal ideation (for example Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale), mania/psychosis warning signs, sleep, mood, blood pressure where relevant; a 24/7 contact route; integration sessions (Track B) or a written list of **independent** integration resources (Track A); plan for post-trial/post-course care before the first session (Brittain et al. 2025 identified post-trial care as a gap); measurement-based outcome tracking (Health Canada SAC 2026). Do not abandon patients who fare badly; terminate only with referral.

**Evidence.** Trial protocols include integration and follow-up; delayed adverse events (mood dips after MDMA; delayed distress after classic psychedelics) are documented (Schlag 2022). Spravato's REMS demonstrates structured post-dose observation (at least 2 hours).

**Criticisms.** Follow-up is under-funded and often unpaid; "integration" is an unregulated label (anyone can claim to be an integration coach); required integration could be a revenue upsell.

## 8. Complaint processes and accountability

**Recommended practice.** Written, plain-language complaint policy given at intake; named person independent of the treating clinician; response timelines; no retaliation or discharge for complaining; explicit statement that patients may complain **directly to the licensing college/board** and, where relevant, to FDA/Health Canada or the state/provincial program regulator, and may report criminal conduct to police; mandatory reporting of colleagues' sexual misconduct as required by law or professional code (CNA 2025; ANA; RHPA mandatory reporting in Ontario); aggregate adverse-event and boundary-incident review at least annually; external (not sponsor-only) safety reporting (FDA PDAC discussion of REMS).

**Evidence.** Regulatory complaint systems are standard; the 2024 FDA discussion and prior journalism indicate internal channels alone failed in a trial setting. Psychedelic-specific data on complaint systems are lacking.

**Criticisms.** Vulnerable patients may not complain; clinics can chill complaints with confidentiality or non-disparagement clauses (avoid them); regulators are slow and psychedelic-specific expertise is thin; peer-led internal reviews can be conflicted.

## 9. Provider wellbeing

**Recommended practice.** Caseload and session-length limits (hours-long sessions are physically and emotionally demanding); debriefing after difficult sessions; access to supervision and, where needed, personal therapy; training in vicarious trauma and secondary stress; protection from financial pressure to overbook; clear policies for provider impairment (no services while impaired; Colorado rule) and for providers who disclose their own distress; fair pay and sustainable staffing for second-person roles. Align with nursing codes' duty to self (ANA 2025 provisions 5–6; CNA 2025) and CMA self-care commitment.

**Evidence.** Direct psychedelic evidence is thin. Extrapolated from psychotherapy research on burnout and personal risk factors (for example Simionato & Simpson 2018, *J Clin Psychol* **[verify]**) and from reports of moral injury after boundary crises in the field.

**Criticisms.** "Wellbeing" language can be used to justify mandatory personal psychedelic experience, which consensus papers reject; limited research means supports are largely untested; resource costs fall on small practices.

## 10. Other practices worth adopting

| Practice | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Public, plain-language **service description** stating Track A or Track B, what is billed, and what is not provided | Prevents billing PAT for monitoring (concept 21) |
| **Conflict-of-interest disclosure** (equity, speaker fees, volume incentives) | Alberta s.4; ABA 1.7 analog; CPHA commercial-determinants concerns |
| **Advertising** limited to evidence-grade claims; no testimonials from suggestible windows | CPSBC advertising standards; HOPE |
| **Records**: tell patients which notes exist and who may compel them | CAP 2025; AMA 3.3.2 |
| **Independent consent-taker** where the patient depends on the clinician (carceral, employment-linked, trial participants) | Helsinki 2024 |
| **Outcome and adverse-event registry participation** | SAC 2026; Brittain 2025 |

## Open questions

- Which of these safeguards reduce harm in practice, and which are rituals? Prospective evaluation (audit data, patient-reported experience) is needed.
- How to reconcile recording with privacy law and subpoenas.
- Whether cooling-off periods help or merely delay access, and for which payment structures.
- How to credential "integration" providers without a profession-wide standard.
