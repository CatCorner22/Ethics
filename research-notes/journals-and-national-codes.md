# Journals and national professional ethics codes

Research inventory for psychedelic-assisted therapy (PAT), consent, authenticity, and related issues, plus national (and federated national) professional ethics codes in the USA, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, with UK documents where they function as international templates.

Not a code, statute, or legal advice. Binding status is as stated by the issuing body; many “codes” bind members of a voluntary association while licensing boards adopt, incorporate, or treat them as evidence of the standard of care.

**Date of this inventory:** 11 November 2026.

**Limits:** Open-web synthesis of published papers and official codes. Paywalled full texts were not always available; summaries rely on abstracts, open-access versions, and consensus reports. ACA 2014 remains current pending a 2026 revision (public comment expected early 2026). CMA announced 2026 wording on racism/discrimination on top of the 2018 Code. AAMFT’s revised Code is effective 1 January 2026; headings below use the still-public 2015/continuing standard structure plus the 2026 effective date. NAADAC/NCC AP Code effective 1 June 2025.

# Part A — Bioethics and philosophy journals

Coverage includes (not limited to) *Hastings Center Report*, *AJOB* / *AJOB Neuroscience*, *Journal of Medical Ethics* (JME), *Bioethics*, *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal* (KIEJ), *Theoretical Medicine and Bioethics*, *Journal of Medicine and Philosophy*, *Philosophy, Psychiatry, & Psychology* (PPP), *Neuroethics*, *Cambridge Quarterly of Healthcare Ethics* (CQHE), *AMA Journal of Ethics*, *Canadian Journal of Bioethics*, *Monash Bioethics Review*, *Journal of Law, Medicine & Ethics* (JLME), *Journal of Bioethical Inquiry* (JBI), *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine*, *Philosophy Compass*, *Journal of Applied Philosophy*, plus adjacent clinical venues (*JAMA Network Open*, *JAMA Psychiatry*, *Psychiatric Services*) when they are the ethics literature of record.

## A1. Transformative experience, consent, and non-ordinary states

### L. A. Paul (2014). *Transformative Experience*. Oxford University Press.
URL: https://global.oup.com/academic/product/transformative-experience-9780198717959

Paul’s book is the philosophical engine of the PAT consent debate even though it is not about psychedelics. An experience is *epistemically* transformative if you cannot know what it is like without undergoing it, and *personally* transformative if it changes your core preferences or identity. Standard expected-utility reasoning then fails: you cannot assign values from your current self to outcomes that will be evaluated by a different self. The book’s critics (Callard on aspiration; later PAT literature) argue that people routinely consent to parenthood, education, and surgery under similar ignorance, so the standard of “full phenomenal understanding” is too strong for medicine.

### Edward Jacobs (2023). Transformative experience and informed consent to psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy. *Frontiers in Psychology*.
DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1108333 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1108333/full

Jacobs applies Paul directly to PAT. Acute mystical/ego-dissolution states and downstream shifts in values, personality, and metaphysics are, he argues, inaccessible at the decision point. Informed consent has two functions: blocking unauthorized bodily trespass, and supporting *values-aligned* choice. Enhanced disclosure may satisfy the first; it cannot satisfy the second, because the values against which the choice is judged may themselves change. He criticizes ordinary consent forms as treating PAT like another psychiatric drug, and warns that preparation itself primes “set,” so the consent conversation is not a value-neutral information dump.

### William R. Smith and Dominic Sisti (2021). Ethics and ego dissolution: the case of psilocybin. *Journal of Medical Ethics* 47(12):807–814.
DOI: 10.1136/medethics-2020-106070 — https://jme.bmj.com/content/47/12/807

Smith and Sisti accept that psilocybin experiences are unlike other psychiatric treatments (ineffability, unity, noetic quality, personality and worldview change) but deny that this makes consent impossible. They propose *enhanced* consent: disclose possible ego dissolution, metaphysical or personality change, mental-health risks (anxiety, trauma re-exposure, rare psychosis), and the possible use of therapeutic touch, with discussion prompts. They criticise moving PAT into “mainstream” clinics without those disclosures, and they flag that research-trial protections will not automatically travel with commercialisation.

### Daniel Villiger (2024). Giving consent to the ineffable. *Neuroethics* 17:11.
DOI: 10.1007/s12152-024-09545-6 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10869409/

Villiger replies to Jacobs. Patients typically know they want to leave a bad status quo (depression, PTSD) and can grasp that PAT is a high-uncertainty, potentially transformative means with a favourable risk–benefit profile in some indications. Phenomenal ignorance of “what it will be like” does not block value-aligned choice if the *function* of the treatment is understood. He criticises Paul’s model as too coarse for clinical PAT, and later (with others) uses Callard’s *aspiration*: patients are agents who reassess views over a course of sessions, not one-shot choosers.

### Brent M. Kious, Andrew Peterson, and colleagues (2024). Are psychedelic experiences transformative? Can we consent to them? *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine*.
DOI: 10.1353/pbm.2024.a919716

Kious et al. argue there is limited evidence that psychedelic experiences are transformative in Paul’s strict sense, and that they may not differ from other medical experiences (surgery, childbirth, chemotherapy) for which consent is uncontroversial. Even if they *are* transformative, consent remains possible. They still recommend consent processes that name the distinctive features (suggestibility, identity talk, touch). They criticise “consent is impossible” as an over-reading that would disable large parts of medicine.

### Andrew Peterson, Emily A. Largent, Holly Fernandez Lynch, Jason Karlawish, Dominic Sisti (2023). Journeying to Ixtlan: ethics of psychedelic medicine and research for Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias. *AJOB Neuroscience* 14(2):107–123.
DOI: 10.1080/21507740.2022.2148771

The authors set a research-ethics agenda for AD/ADRD: autonomy and consent under fluctuating capacity; ego dissolution colliding with a pathology of self; caregiver burden and influence; exploitation of desperation; IRB unfamiliarity; inequity. They criticise both naive exclusion of people with dementia (protecting them out of potential benefit) and naive inclusion without extra safeguards. Comments in the same issue (e.g. Poppe & Trachsel; Rosenbaum on MAID overlap) press palliative framing and coercion risk.

### Tahlia R. Lee and colleagues (2024). Informed consent to psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy: ethical considerations. (open review / *Canadian Journal of Psychiatry* cluster; PMC11032091).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11032091/

Lee et al. largely follow Smith & Sisti and add three operational tools: (1) advance directives for PAT (touch, music, visitors, metaphysics-neutrality) while capacitous; (2) *dynamic* consent across preparation, dosing, and integration rather than a single signature; (3) a trustful relationship *before* the first drug session. They criticise one-time consent forms that ignore suggestibility after ingestion.

### Daniel Villiger (2024). Informed Consent and Transformative Experiences in Psychedelic Therapy: A Nuanced Approach. *AJOB* (open peer commentary).
DOI: 10.1080/15265161.2025.2509929

Commentary agreeing with Villiger that Paul’s ignorance is overstated. Patients should be treated as agents who interpret and integrate, not as passive subjects of a single event. Extra caution when assumptions that “sustain a sense of security” will be challenged. Criticises both “consent is impossible” and purely legalistic forms that omit aspiration, life-narrative, and belief-flexibility.

### Conceptual analysis (2026). Agency, informed consent and transformative experiences: a proposed conceptual analysis of the ethical challenges of psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy. *Journal of Psychedelic Studies*.
DOI: 10.1556/2054.2026.00562

Authors split transformation into trigger (acute session), interpretative process (integration), and longitudinal outcomes. Agency is most compromised during the acute phase and more intact in preparation and later reflection. They criticise “psychedelic ethical exceptionalism”: regretted identity change is real, but it does not require a unique consent doctrine—rather, enhanced disclosure of transformative *uncertainty*, protection during susceptibility, and follow-up that supports agency after change.

### Daniel Villiger (2025). Psychedelic ethics. *Philosophy Compass*.
DOI: 10.1111/phc3.70059 — https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phc3.70059

Field map: consent (Jacobs vs Smith/Sisti vs Kious vs Villiger); safety and therapist influence; research design/unblinding; personal-use requirements; metaphysics and the “comforting delusion” objection; medicalization vs spiritual use. Criticises hype (“Pollan effect”), historical amnesia about 1950s–60s abuses, and the tendency of commercial PAT to outrun ethics.

## A2. Authenticity, identity change, and belief

### G. A. Nielson (2022). Knowing and being known: psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy and the sense of authenticity. *Frontiers in Psychiatry*.
DOI: 10.3389/fpsyt.2022.933495

Nielson relocates “authenticity” from a property of drug-induced mystical states to an *intersubjective* event: feeling of knowing and being known when defensive barriers drop with the therapist (and imagined others). Draws on Winnicott (True Self, transitional space), implicit relational knowing, and REBUS/relaxed beliefs. Criticises purely epistemic debates (“was the vision true?”) for missing how authenticity is *felt* in relationship—and therefore how easily a therapist can colonise that feeling.

### Neşe Devenot, Aidan Seale-Feldman, Elyse Smith, Tehseen Noorani, Albert Garcia-Romeu, Matthew W. Johnson (2022). Psychedelic identity shift: a critical approach to set and setting. *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal* 32(4):359–399.
DOI: 10.1353/ken.2022.0022

The authors argue that “set and setting” is not a neutral container. Clinical scripts, music, art, therapist metaphysics, and trial branding *steer* identity. They criticise the field’s claim to non-directiveness while running highly curated mystical-type protocols, and they warn that identity shift can be iatrogenic (political, religious, or relational rupture) as well as therapeutic.

### Christopher Timmermann et al. (2021). Psychedelics alter metaphysical beliefs. *Scientific Reports* 11:22166.
DOI: 10.1038/s41598-021-01209-2

Prospective survey plus a controlled trial: post-use shifts away from physicalism toward panpsychism and (acutely) fatalism, lasting ≥6 months, correlated with past use and mental-health improvement, moderated by impressionability, mediated by emotional synchrony. The paper itself is empirical; its ethical upshot (taken up by Letheby, HOPE, APA psychiatry) is that belief change is a foreseeable risk/benefit that consent and therapist neutrality must address. Critics note online-sample bias and that “improved mental health” does not settle whether the new metaphysics is *true* or merely comforting.

### Chris Letheby (2021). *Philosophy of Psychedelics*. Oxford University Press.
Letheby’s “naturalistic entheogenics” answers the *comforting delusion objection*: even if mystical metaphysics is false, therapeutic benefit may ride on *self-representation* change, not on acquiring cosmic knowledge. He still treats unmitigated epistemic cost as ethically serious (Nozick’s experience machine). Critics (Lavazza; some Indigenous and religious scholars) say this recentres Western naturalism as the epistemic court of appeal; Flanagan & Graham (2017) and McMillan (2021) reply that flourishing can outweigh epistemic purity.

### David B. Yaden, Brian D. Earp, Roland R. Griffiths (2022). Ethical issues regarding nonsubjective psychedelics as standard of care. *Cambridge Quarterly of Healthcare Ethics* 31(4):464–471.
DOI: 10.1017/s096318012200007x

If the therapeutic action of psychedelics partly *is* the subjective experience, then “nonsubjective” analogues (same biology, no trip) should be reserved for patients for whom the trip is contraindicated, not made the default. They criticise a research pipeline that treats meaning, awe, and mystical-type experience as noise to be engineered out—an ethical withholding of typically valued experience.

### Kurt Rasmussen and David E. Olson (2022). Psychedelics as standard of care? Many questions remain. *CQHE* 31(4):477–481.
DOI: 10.1017/s096318012200010x

Reply: the science of *whether* subjective effects are necessary is unsettled; premature “classic psychedelics as default” risks locking in a high-resource, high-vulnerability care model. They criticise ethical arguments that outrun mechanistic evidence.

### O. Thomas and F. Silva (2025). Authenticity and love drugs: insights from psychedelic therapy. *AJOB Neuroscience*.
DOI: 10.1080/21507740.2025.2517007

Commentary: lay moral disgust at “love drugs” as inauthentic misfires. Psychedelic therapy shows that chemically scaffolded emotion can still be meaningful if it supports lasting, value-aligned change. They criticise a natural/artificial binary that would also condemn SSRIs and, inconsistently, PAT.

### Christopher Timmermann, Rosalind Watts, David Dupuis (2022). Towards psychedelic apprenticeship: developing a gentle touch for the mediation and validation of psychedelic-induced insights. *Frontiers in Psychology*.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9660279/

Noetic confidence plus suggestibility is a double-edged sword: it may drive therapy and also false memory, guru capture, and commercially steered “insights.” They propose *apprenticeship*—validation practices based on empathic resonance by an experienced guide—going beyond generic “prep and integration.” They criticise both uncritical endorsement of revelations and a purely dismissive “it’s just the drug” stance (which they and later writers such as Zeller 2024 treat as a form of epistemic injustice).

## A3. Therapist power, suggestibility, REBUS, and exploitation

### Daniel Villiger and Manuel Trachsel (2023). With great power comes great vulnerability: an ethical analysis of psychedelics’ therapeutic mechanisms proposed by the REBUS hypothesis. *Journal of Medical Ethics* 49(12):826–832.
DOI: 10.1136/jme-2022-108816

If REBUS is right, psychedelics relax high-level priors and increase bottom-up influence: patients become highly suggestible and setting-dependent. That is the proposed *mechanism of benefit* and the mechanism of harm. Historical sexual and ideological exploitation is therefore not an accident but a predictable misuse of the mechanism. They demand enhanced consent about this vulnerability and extra security measures (two therapists, limits on interpretation during the session). They criticise protocols that tell patients to “trust, let go, be open” without disclosing that this maxim increases dependence on the therapist.

### Sarah McNamee, Neşe Devenot, Meaghan Buisson (2023). Studying harms is key to improving psychedelic-assisted therapy—participants call for changes to research landscape. *JAMA Psychiatry* 80(5):411.
DOI: 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2023.0099

Participant-authors: adverse events in PAT trials are poorly defined and under-reported; the *understudied therapeutic component*—therapist–patient interaction under high-dose psychedelics—is the most serious risk class. They criticise sponsor-captured harm assessment, directing complaints first to the PI, and the field’s reluctance to fund independent, interdisciplinary harms research. Buisson’s MAPS MDMA-trial experience (sexual assault by an unlicensed co-therapist) is the lived-experience core.

### Tahlia Harrison (2025). Wolves among sheep: sexual violations in psychedelic-assisted therapy. (working paper / collected essays).
http://sonyafaber.com/papers/Harrison_WolvesSheepPAT_2025.pdf

Historical and contemporary cases (including MAPS-adjacent and underground). Criticises: ethics codes that arrived late and enforce weakly; MAPS training manuals that prohibit sexual behaviour but do not specify *when* touch-consent is obtained or what training authorises bodywork; complaint pathways that run through the investigator; unlicensed co-therapists outside any college; underground practice with no accountability at all.

### Daniel Villeneuve and David Prescott (2022). Examining the dark sides of psychedelic therapy.
https://www.davidprescott.net/articles/2022Villeneuve_%20Prescott_Psychedelics.pdf

No circumstance makes sexual contact therapeutic; the client cannot consent to it. They criticise MAPS for condemning Yensen while taking little restorative action; for a two-therapist protocol in which only one need be licensed; for a Code that says “never abandon” while some harmed participants report being ignored; and for scientific bias against results that falsify the hypothesis.

### Daniel Villiger (2024). How to make psychedelic-assisted therapy safer. *Cambridge Quarterly of Healthcare Ethics*.
https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/48FBF66C57C389EC84D1C396E4DD55BF/S0963180124000604a.pdf/how_to_make_psychedelicassisted_therapy_safer.pdf

Practical safety: consider allowing a trusted other in the room as a monitor *if* that does not disturb the session; take a precautionary line on touch until better evidence exists. Criticises safety literature that names suggestibility but stays silent on operational safeguards.

### Gregory Barber and Charles C. Dike (2023). Ethical and practical considerations for the use of psychedelics in psychiatry. *Psychiatric Services* 74(8):838–846.
DOI: 10.1176/appi.ps.20220525

Clinical-psychiatry synthesis: evidence still limited; unblinding; therapist influence; equity; off-label ketamine exuberance; need for training standards. Criticises both prohibitionist delay and commercial clinic gold-rush.

### Brent M. Kious, Zachary T. Schwartz, Benjamin R. Lewis (2022). Should we be leery of being Leary? Concerns about psychedelic use by psychedelic researchers. *Journal of Psychopharmacology* 37(1):45–48.
DOI: 10.1177/02698811221133461

Personal use by researchers may produce excess enthusiasm, confirmatory bias, and community capture. They do not ban private use but criticise treating personal experience as a scientific credential.

### Nathan Emmerich and Bryce Humphries (2024). Is the requirement for first-person experience of psychedelic drugs a justified component of a psychedelic therapist’s training? *Cambridge Quarterly of Healthcare Ethics* 33(4):548–557.

They argue “must have taken the drug” is not justified: comparable epistemic access may come from meditation, holotropic breathwork, or sensory deprivation, and there is no evidence that personal use improves outcomes or reduces harm. Villiger (2024c) concurs that evidence is insufficient. Banbury/McGuire consensus: personal experience should *not* be required for licensure.

## A4. Touch, sexual boundaries, and the body

### Brian D. Earp, David B. Yaden, et al. / HOPE workshop (2024). The Hopkins-Oxford Psychedelic Ethics (HOPE) Consensus Statement. Also discussed in *AJOB*.
https://www.hope-statement.com/report.pdf

Ten sections (Indigenous position; precautionary science; diverse motivations; education; consent; equity; professional conduct; special vulnerability/abuse; breadth of research; communication ethics). On touch: sexual touch never; the group *divided* on therapeutic/supportive touch when non-touch comfort exists; honour any refusal; two-stage consent (before and during acute effects) as a precautionary option. Criticises hype, WEIRD samples, privacy-violating “real world evidence,” and forgetting MK-Ultra, prisoner abuse, and sexual abuse.

### Amy L. McGuire, I. Glenn Cohen, Dominic Sisti, et al. (2024). Developing an ethics and policy framework for psychedelic clinical care: a consensus statement. *JAMA Network Open* 7(6):e2414650. (Banbury / Cold Spring Harbor.)
DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.14650 — https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2819456

Twenty consensus points in five issue areas: reparations/reciprocity/equity/respect; informed consent (including non-response and identity change; consent *as set-priming*); professional boundaries and physical touch (safety touch always; sexual never; supportive touch by shared decision-making; mid-session refusal honoured; conservative honoring of *pre-dose* refusal if the patient “changes their mind” while intoxicated); personal experience not required for licensure; gatekeeping (training, insurance, DEA/FDA). Disagreements recorded rather than papered over.

### Qualitative study (2025/2026). The ethical use of therapeutic touch in psychedelic-assisted therapy: a qualitative study of researcher perspectives. *Neuroethics* / PMC12618852.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12618852/

Interviewees: therapist must uphold *pre-established* boundaries during dosing. Four challenges: boundary transgressions; withholding touch that the patient now wants; challenges to *therapists’* boundaries; power dynamics. Consent treated as a process; need body-map specificity including atypical erogenous zones; train on nonverbal withdrawal. Criticises flexible “alliance” consent that slides into boundary drift after dosing (echoed by McHerron et al. 2025).

### Practical guidelines for supportive touch in PAT and MDMA-AT (2026). *Neuroethics*.
DOI: 10.1007/s12152-026-09631-x

Conservative model: two-stage consent-assent (ordinary consciousness vs session); clear categories of touch; ongoing assessment; alternatives when touch is contraindicated; cultural sensitivity and power. Criticises existing protocols for a gap between “touch is important” and operational ethics.

### MAPS/Lykos and FSMB (context, not journals).
Phase 2 documented therapists in bed with a participant; FDA PDAC (4 June 2024) treated this as a benefit–risk ethics issue, not a one-off HR problem. FSMB (2020): sexual behaviour between physician and patient is never diagnostic or therapeutic, including conduct a patient could reasonably construe as sexual.

## A5. Indigenous knowledge, medicalization, epistemic injustice, spiritual bypass

### Yuria Celidwen, Nicole Redvers, et al. (2023). Ethical principles of traditional Indigenous medicine to guide western psychedelic research and practice. *The Lancet Regional Health – Americas* 18:100410.
DOI: 10.1016/j.lana.2022.100410 — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanam/article/PIIS2667-193X(22)00227-7/fulltext

Indigenous-led consensus: eight Rs in four clusters — Acknowledgement (Reverence, Respect); Knowledge (Responsibility, Relevance); IP (Regulation, Reparation); Belonging (Restoration, Reconciliation). Concrete criticisms: token Indigenous presence; enclosing patents; extractive tourism and spiritual consumerism; unsustainable harvest; cartel violence in source territories; “one pill to enlightenment” narratives; the very term “psychedelics” as a Western reduction of Spirit medicines. Clinic-level asks: FPIC, benefit-sharing, no enclosure, ecosystem care.

### Eduardo E. Schenberg and colleagues. Overcoming epistemic injustices in the biomedical study of ayahuasca: towards ethical and sustainable regulation.
(Consciousness-library / open version of the argument.)

Questions the epistemic authority of double-blind molecular trials over millennia of Indigenous therapeutic use. Criticises molecularization, safety discourse that ignores ceremonial context, and medicalization-at-scale that could harm Amazonian peoples and ecosystems approaching a dieback tipping point. Demands ILO 169 FPIC, cultural-heritage protections against patents, and CBD benefit-sharing for isolated compounds.

### Maastricht Journal of Liberal Arts (student/STS). The medicalisation of ayahuasca as a depression treatment: cultural reductivism and biopiracy.

STS reading: neuroreductivism (Martin) plus biopiracy. Hierarchy of scientific over Indigenous knowledge may even *reduce* effectiveness by stripping context.

### HOPE §I and Banbury “reparations and reciprocity.”
Both criticise medicalization that treats Indigenous groups as historical footnotes or branding. HOPE notes disagreement about how far modern clinical PAT is co-optation vs adaptation, but consensus on restitution, respect, reciprocity, and benefit-sharing.

### “Spiritual bypass” (clinical and critical literature).
Not a single canonical ethics-journal article; the concept (Welwood) is used in PAT critique to name using mystical experience to avoid trauma work, relational repair, or political injustice—and to name Western consumers’ use of Indigenous ceremony as a wellness product. Timmermann’s apprenticeship paper and Celidwen’s “spiritual consumerism” are the closest journal-grade treatments. McNamee/Devenot add that “integration” rhetoric can itself bypass documented harm.

### Zeller (2024) (as mapped by Villiger 2025 Compass).
Treating patients’ increased non-naturalistic beliefs as simply *false* is epistemic injustice (Fricker) toward their meaning-making. Push toward epistemic pluralism in the therapy room—tension with Letheby’s naturalism and with anti-capture duties (do not install the therapist’s religion).

## A6. Research ethics, Helsinki, FDA/MDMA, unblinding

### World Medical Association (2024). Declaration of Helsinki (revised).
Plain-language consent including funding, COI, incentives, and harm compensation; independent consent-takers when the participant is in a dependent relationship; placebo only if methodologically necessary and not exposing people to additional serious irreversible harm. Directly relevant to PAT trials with dependent patient–therapist dyads and to placebo/unblinding design.

### State-of-the-field review (2025). Ethics of psychedelics in clinical research. *JME / BMJ* companion (*jmepb*).
https://jmepb.bmj.com/content/2/4/e000035

Maps consent-impossibility vs consent-possible camps; lists enhanced-consent items (perceptual change, personality/value change, abuse/exploitation, therapeutic touch; special populations: military, end of life, under-18). Notes that if PAT consent is truly impossible, DBS and major cosmetic surgery are in the same boat.

### FDA Psychopharmacologic Drugs Advisory Committee, 4 June 2024 (Lykos MDMA) — ethics commentary.

Votes: 9–2 data did not show PTSD efficacy; 10–1 benefits did not outweigh risks. Ethics inside benefit–risk: functional unblinding (~90% on drug, ~75% on placebo guessed assignment); therapist suggestibility and sponsor-only training; documented sexual misconduct; impairment lasting beyond the session; incomplete cardiac/QT and lab data; hyponatremia; abuse/diversion; REMS debate (onsite medical staff; *external* safety reporting).

### Perspective (2025). A reflection on paradigmatic tensions within the FDA advisory committee for MDMA-assisted therapy. PMC11967080.

The FDA says it does not regulate psychotherapy, yet counted psychotherapeutic sexual-violation risk in the drug’s benefit–risk. That paradox sits at the edge of a biological-psychiatry paradigm (drug effect = molecule; context = noise) versus an interactional paradigm (drug × setting). Some FDA critiques of MAPS/Lykos methods are granted as valid; others are paradigm clash.

### Critical appraisal (2025). Psychedelic-assisted therapy — supposedly paradigm-shifting research with poor attempts at hypotheses falsifying and questionable ethics. PMC11713989.

Uses Beauchamp & Childress: suppressing adverse-event knowledge violates non-maleficence and distorts beneficence. Cites ICER’s darker reading of MAPP1/MAPP2; MDMA neurotoxicity literature; Australia’s TGA authorised-prescriber path vs FDA rejection.

### American Journal of Psychiatry Residents’ Journal (2024). New territory: ethical challenges associated with MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD.
https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp-rj.2024.200108

FDA’s problem: combination product (drug + psychotherapy) with no clean device/drug analogue. REMS/ETASU may be used to mandate psychotherapy training—raising access vs safety. Draft FDA psychedelic-trial guidance worries that psychotherapy *increases* expectancy and performance bias. Off-label use after any approval.

### Aday, Muthukumaraswamy, and others (2021–2023). Expectancy, unblinding, and “rescuing” RCTs. *Psychopharmacology* and related.

Functional unblinding is structural. Proposed fixes (recruit only naive participants; conceal information) themselves raise consent and deception issues under Helsinki and Common Rule.

### Lauren S. Otterman (2022). Research into psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy for anorexia nervosa should be funded. *Journal of Bioethical Inquiry* 20(1):31–39.
DOI: 10.1007/s11673-022-10220-9

Justice argument for including a high-need, high-risk diagnosis; criticises conservative exclusion that concentrates evidence in depression/PTSD.

### Sisti et al. (2023). Past is prologue: ethical issues in pediatric psychedelics research and treatment. *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine* 66(1):129–144.

Almost no developmental data; exceptional-benefit-only bar; assent plus parental permission; long-horizon identity effects.

## A7. Ketamine, microdosing, enhancement, and “support vs psychotherapy”

### Journal of Psychedelic Psychiatry / field guidelines. Ethical guidelines for ketamine clinicians.
Three roles every therapeutic-ketamine encounter: mental-health professional (intake, prep, in-session support, integration, psych emergency); medical professional (physical safety); patient. One person may hold both professional roles only if actually qualified in both. Ketamine is dissociative/psychedelic, not “just” anesthesia. Criticises medical spas that omit psychological care and psychotherapists who omit medical rescue.

### Ketamine Psychotherapy Associates (2024). KAP Code of Ethics.
https://ketaminepsychotherapyassociates.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/KAP-Code-of-Ethics-7-21.pdf

Alliance, inner healing intelligence, autonomy of meaning-making, no dual relationships, touch only with consent and never sexual, sexual contact with participant/former participant/family prohibited during and after, countertransference examination. Essentially a MAPS-style code ported to legal ketamine.

### Petrie-Flom / Bill of Health (2022). Restricting access to microdosing is morally wrong.
Autonomy, flourishing, harm-reduction: if low-dose use has plausible benefit and low harm, criminalization is unjust; but the same essay flags classist retreats, racialized drug-law fear, and quality-control failures. Not a peer-reviewed journal article; included because it is the main public microdosing-*ethics* claim.

### Macrodosing to microdosing (review). Clinical, social, and cultural perspectives.
Ethics of microdosing: inadequate RCTs; self-protocols; placebo; cognitive-enhancement fairness (workplace/school “doping”); cultural appropriation even at sub-perceptual doses; do-no-harm given unsupervised distress. Ibogaine literature (Brunt 2026, as cited in prior research in this repo): microdosing does not waive cardiac screening.

### Goodwin and colleagues vs PAT orthodoxy (2023–2025). Psychological *support* versus psychotherapy.

Some trialists argue that what was done in successful trials is presence and safety, not a named psychotherapy, and that requiring psychotherapy restricts access and confounds the drug effect. Richard & Levine (2025) propose observable microskills to tell support from therapy. Ethical stakes: billing, competence, Track A vs Track B (this repository’s framework), and FDA’s worry that therapy inflates expectancy.

### CPHA (2024) public-health discussion (Canada).
Commercial investment outrunning evidence; Indigenous misappropriation; trial and clinic equity; regulate the illegal market that already exists (storefronts, microdosing, underground guides); apply alcohol/tobacco/cannabis lessons to psychedelic clinics.

## A8. Scope of medicine, access, special populations, MAID, military

## A9. Exceptionalism, hype, and criticisms of the field’s ethics

### AJOB (2024). Distinctive but not exceptional: the risks of psychedelic ethical exceptionalism.
DOI: 10.1080/15265161.2024.2433421

Analogy to genetic exceptionalism: distinctive features do not justify a separate ethical or evidentiary *standard*. If standards should change, change them for all relevant medicine, not by appealing to uniqueness. Criticises both “psychedelics are so special they skip RCTs” and “so special they cannot be consented to.”

### AJOB (2025). Psychedelic medicine exceptionalism.
DOI: 10.1080/15265161.2025.2434398

Analytic survey of the exceptionalism debate for regulation, commercialisation, and administration.

### Yaden, Earp, Griffiths and others on hype.
Overly positive media (the “Pollan effect”) distorts consent by inflating expected benefit and deflating harm—itself an ethics problem, not just a PR problem. HOPE §X: communication ethics.

### Brittain et al. (August 2025). *Journal of Bioethical Inquiry* scoping review of 51 articles.
Clusters: standards of practice, equity, integrity, cultural appropriation, epistemic justice. Workshop-identified gaps: **post-trial care**, no consensus on care models or practitioner competencies, weak translation of research ethics into ordinary prescribing.

### ICER reviews of MDMA-AT.
Independent health-technology assessment used by critics to argue that published benefit was cherry-picked relative to harms, functional unblinding, and misconduct.

## Medicine

### AMA *Code of Medical Ethics* — USA — **binding on AMA members**; widely treated as the profession’s national ethics statement and used in litigation/board actions — Principles 2001; modernized Code 2016; opinions through 2024

**Categories (Code’s own architecture):** nine *Principles of Medical Ethics*; then 11 opinion chapters.

**Principles (concepts):** competence and compassion; professionalism and honesty; respect for law; rights and confidentiality; scientific method and consultation; freedom of choice of practice (except emergencies); public health; **patient welfare paramount**; access to care.

**Opinion chapters and concepts:**

- **Patient–physician relationships:** covenant of trust; non-discrimination of prospective patients; emergency care duty; **non-abandonment** / continuity; termination procedures; sexual/romantic relations with current patients prohibited; industry influence.
- **Consent, communication, decision making:** **informed consent** (Opinion 2.1.1, 2024 update); capacity; documentation; surrogate decision-making; pediatric assent; withholding/withdrawing.
- **Privacy, confidentiality, and medical records:** minimum-necessary; harm and **mandatory-reporting** exceptions; electronic records (3.3.2).
- **Research and innovation:** IRB, consent to research, innovative treatment vs research.
- **Genetics and reproductive medicine.**
- **Organ procurement and transplantation.**
- **Medical education and trainees.**
- **Physicians’ relationships with other professionals and institutions:** consultation; impaired colleagues (**impairment**); reporting.
- **Financing, fees, gifts, advertising:** honest **advertising**; conflicts; stewardship.
- **Physician exercise of conscience:** limited; emergencies and non-discrimination still bind.
- **Quality and professionalism / public health.**

**Psychedelic relevance:** AMA supplies the medical floor (indication honesty, capacitous consent, confidentiality, non-abandonment, sexual prohibition, research vs off-label). It does **not** mention touch, NOSC, or identity change. Critics: the Code is association-based not statute; 2016 modernization was charged with being hard to use; conscience and reproductive opinions are politically contested; it is silent on suggestibility.

### CMA *Code of Ethics and Professionalism* (2018; 2026 wording on racism/discrimination) — Canada — **recommended national framework**; provincial colleges are the binding regulators — 2018

**Categories:** (A) Virtues; (B) Fundamental commitments; (C) Professional responsibilities (to patient; to self; to colleagues; to society), plus the 2026 anti-racism additions.

**Concepts:**

- **Virtues:** compassion, honesty, humility, integrity, prudence; trust as cornerstone.
- **Commitments:** well-being of the patient; respect for persons (**never exploit**); justice; integrity and **competence**; collaboration; self-care; inquiry and reflection.
- **To the patient:** consider first the patient’s well-being; prevent/minimize harm; disclose harm; **informed consent** and capacity; **confidentiality**; non-discrimination; **cultural safety** / anti-racism (2026); appropriate boundaries (**sexual-boundary**, **dual-relationship** via “never exploit”).
- **To self:** **impairment**, wellness, limits of skill (humility).
- **To colleagues:** respectful collaboration; report unsafe practice.
- **To society:** stewardship, public health, research integrity (**research**).

**Psychedelic relevance:** “Never exploit” + humility + consent is the Canadian medical hook for anti-capture and experimental-status disclosure. Binding force is college policies (CPSA, CPSBC ketamine, CPSO CAM), not the CMA text.

### AMA Australia *Code of Ethics* (2004; editorial 2006; revised 2016) — Australia — **binding on AMA members**; AHPRA/Medical Board *Good Medical Practice* is the **mandatory** registration standard — 2016

**Categories:** Preamble; Patient care; Professional independence / colleagues; Society; Research.

**Concepts:** patient well-being first; dignity, compassion, culturally and linguistically appropriate care (**cultural safety**); free choice of doctor; **informed consent** (emergency exception); **confidentiality**; **competence** and referral; no exploitation; honesty in **advertising**; teaching; **research** ethics; public health; end-of-life.

**Psychedelic relevance:** TGA Authorised Prescriber + RANZCP sit on top of this floor. Take-home psychedelics not anticipated.

### NZMA *Code of Ethics* — New Zealand — **recommended** (NZMA; the Association has wound down as a membership body; the Code remains a referenced ethics document) — latest widely circulated edition with 12 principles

**Categories:** 12 Principles of Ethical Behaviour, then annotated guidance (patient care, confidentiality, colleagues, society, research).

**Concepts:** patient health first; rights, autonomy, relationships, free choice; trust, **no exploitation**; moral integrity, compassion, dignity; **confidentiality** in life and after death (public-interest/safety override); **competence**; scientific basis and responsible innovation (**research**); honour the profession; recognise limits and others’ skills; community health; advocate for resources; maintain professional standards. **Sexual-boundary** via non-exploitation.

### Medical Council of New Zealand *Good Medical Practice* — New Zealand — **mandatory** (HPCAA s.118; also Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers’ Rights, Right 4) — current edition

**Categories (not a “code of ethics” — the Council says so):** professional values; providing good care; working with patients (**informed consent**, **confidentiality**, **cultural safety** including Māori); working with colleagues; teaching/supervision; **research**; health of the doctor (**impairment**); raising concerns (**mandatory reporting** to the Council).

**Psychedelic relevance:** Cultural safety and the HDC Code (informed consent as a *legal right*) are stricter operational hooks than NZMA’s aspirational principles.

### UK GMC *Good Medical Practice* (2024) — United Kingdom — **mandatory** for licensed doctors; international template for “good medical practice” documents — 2024

**Categories:** knowledge, skills, development; patients, partnership, communication; colleagues, culture, safety; maintaining trust (honesty, **advertising**/conflicts, candour).

**Concepts:** **competence**; **informed consent**; **confidentiality**; **sexual-boundary** (GMC has a dedicated sexual-boundaries statement); **impairment**; speaking up; non-discrimination. **Touch** not PAT-specific.

**Psychedelic relevance:** Template for how regulators write *enforceable* practice standards as opposed to association ethics codes. UK has no licensed PAT analogue at national scale comparable to TGA AP.

## Psychiatry

### APA (American Psychiatric Association) *Principles of Medical Ethics with Annotations Especially Applicable to Psychiatry* — USA — **binding on APA members**; used by boards — current annotations on AMA Principles

**Categories:** the nine AMA Principles with psychiatry annotations; plus position statements and resource documents.

**Concepts:** **confidentiality** (including after death, extra caution); **sexual-boundary** (never with current patients; former patients almost always unethical); **dual-relationship**; exploitation of transference; **informed consent** including to psychotherapy; **competence** in biological *and* psychotherapeutic methods; **abandonment**; **impairment**; gold-watering **advertising**; **research** on vulnerable mentally ill persons; **mandatory reporting** vs privilege.

**Psychedelic-specific APA documents (not the Code, but national specialty ethics):**

- Position: inadequate evidence to endorse psychedelics for any psychiatric disorder except approved investigational studies; no ballot-initiative medicine.
- Resource document: equipoise; unblinding; **enhanced consent** (possible change in personality, politics, metaphysics, religion); vulnerability to sexual/financial exploitation; **double consent for touch**; no prescribing for self-improvement/recreation; harm-reduction talk without assisting illegal use; Indigenous ceremonial distinction; ketamine-clinic caution; diagnosable condition + functional impairment required.

### CPA (Canadian Psychiatric Association) — Canada — **recommended** professional guidance; colleges bind — position papers rather than a unique numbered ethics code (physicians use CMA + provincial)

**Concepts:** overlapping CMA; extra on involuntary treatment, capacity, dual loyalty.

**Psychedelic relevance:** CANMAT 2023 (serotonergic psychedelics for MDD: experimental; trials first; SAP rare). Health Canada SAC (through 2026): investigational; established options first.

### RANZCP *Code of Ethics* (5th ed. 2018; PDF still issued 2025) — Australia & New Zealand — **binding on RANZCP members** (Fellows, trainees, CPD); **recommended** for non-member psychiatrists; regulators may use as benchmark — 2018

**Categories — 11 principles:**

1. Humanity, dignity, autonomy (non-discrimination; **do not impose values** — **cultural safety**).
2. **Shall not exploit** patients (**sexual-boundary**, **dual-relationship**, financial).
3. Best attainable care (**competence**).
4. Privacy and **confidentiality** of patients and families.
5. Valid **informed consent** (including reports).
6. Shall not misuse professional knowledge and skills.
7. Clinical **research** — NHMRC and international guidelines.
8. Maintain and share knowledge (teaching).
9. Own and colleagues’ health (**impairment**).
10. Integrity of the medical profession (**advertising**/honesty).
11. Improve services; anti-stigma; community.

**Psychedelic relevance:** TGA AP pathway: specialist psychiatrists; MDMA for PTSD; psilocybin for TRD; NHMRC-registered HREC protocol; psychiatrist overall responsibility; training or close supervision by someone who has treated at least one similar patient. Code’s “do not impose values” and “do not exploit” are the PAT hooks. **Touch** not named.

## Psychology

### APA *Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct* (2017) — USA — **binding on APA members**; many state boards adopt or adapt — 2002/2003 with 2010 and 2017 amendments; full rewrite in progress historically

**Aspirational principles (not enforceable):** A Beneficence and Nonmaleficence; B Fidelity and Responsibility; C Integrity; D Justice; E Respect for People’s Rights and Dignity.

**Enforceable standards (categories):**

1. **Resolving ethical issues** — conflicts, informal resolution, reporting, cooperating with ethics committees.
2. **Competence** — boundaries of competence; **10.01(b)-adjacent**: unestablished techniques require extra disclosure; **impairment**; personal problems.
3. **Human relations** — unfair discrimination; harassment; **avoid harm**; **multiple relationships (dual-relationship)**; conflict of interest; third-party requests; exploitative relationships; cooperation; informed consent; interruption of services.
4. **Privacy and confidentiality** — limits, recording, disclosures, consultations.
5. **Advertising and other public statements** — no false statements; **testimonials** restrictions; no in-person solicitation.
6. **Record keeping and fees** — barter caution; referrals and fees.
7. **Education and training** — sexual relationships with students/supervisees prohibited.
8. **Research and publication** — IRB, consent, deception, debriefing, animals.
9. **Assessment.**
10. **Therapy** — **informed consent** to therapy; couples/families (who is the client); groups; **sexual intimacies with current clients banned**; relatives of current clients banned; former sexual partners; former clients (typically **at least two years** plus burden on the psychologist to prove no exploitation); interruption; **terminating / non-abandonment**.

**Touch:** not named. **Cultural:** Principle E and 3.01. **Mandatory reporting:** via 4.05 and law.

**Criticisms of this Code:** post-Hoffman Report (interrogation/ethics); O’Donohue and others — risk-management tone, moral thinness, enforcement theatre; Teo — cannot handle epistemological violence, war-on-terror ideology, or DSM financial COI; “not exhaustive” clauses create loopholes.

**Psychedelic relevance:** 10.01(b) unestablished-technique disclosure; 3.05 dual roles (prescriber-therapist); 5.05 testimonials (noetic-period quotes); 10.05–10.08 sexual rules (weaker than MAPS “never” and CRPO five-year +).

### CPA *Canadian Code of Ethics for Psychologists* (4th ed., 2017) — Canada — **binding on CPA members**; most provincial regulatory colleges adopt it or a close variant as **mandatory** — 2017

**Categories — four weighted principles** (except imminent bodily harm):

- **I Dignity of Persons and Peoples** — respect, rights, non-discrimination, fair process, **informed consent**, **freedom of consent**, protections for vulnerable groups, privacy, **confidentiality**, extended responsibility.
- **II Responsible Caring** — general caring, **competence** and self-knowledge, risk/benefit, maximize benefit, minimize/offset harm, animals.
- **III Integrity in Relationships** — honesty, lack of bias, openness, incomplete disclosure/deception, **conflict of interest / dual-relationship**, reliance on the discipline.
- **IV Responsibility to Society** — knowledge, beneficial activities, respect for society, development of society.

**Psychedelic relevance:** Freedom of consent + vulnerable-group protection is the strongest national psychology hook for NOSC. Sexual specifics live more in provincial standards (e.g. CAP PAPT 2025).

### APS *Code of Ethics* (2007, guidelines updated) — Australia — **binding on APS members**; PsyBA/AHPRA Code of Conduct is **mandatory** for registration — 2007

**Categories:** General Principle A Respect for the rights and dignity of people and peoples; B Propriety; C Integrity — each with explanatory and practice standards.

**Concepts:** **informed consent**; **confidentiality**; **competence**; non-exploitation (**sexual-boundary**, **dual-relationship**); **research**; Indigenous psychological practice (**cultural safety**); **advertising**; **impairment**; **mandatory reporting** under National Law.

### NZPsS / NZ Psychologists Board / NZCCP *Code of Ethics for Psychologists Working in Aotearoa New Zealand* — New Zealand — **profession-wide ethics code** (recommended/educational); **NZPB Code of Conduct applies to registered psychologists from 1 October 2026 (mandatory)** — current ethics code; Conduct 2026

**Categories (ethics code):** Respect for dignity of persons and peoples; Responsible caring; Integrity in relationships; Social justice and responsibility to society (Treaty of Waitangi / Te Tiriti framing).

**Concepts:** **informed consent**; **confidentiality**; **competence**; **cultural safety** (Māori as tangata whenua); **dual-relationship**; **sexual-boundary**; **research**; **impairment**.

**Psychedelic relevance:** Te Tiriti and cultural safety constrain medicalization of rongoā and imported PAT protocols.

- **III Professional Competence and Integrity** — **competence**; **impairment**; no harassment; accurate representation; **research** integrity; no undue influence.
- **IV Responsibility to Students and Supervisees** — no sexual with supervisees.
- **V Research and Publication.**
- **VI Technology-Assisted Professional Services.**
- **VII Professional Evaluations** (forensic).
- **VIII Financial Arrangements** — fees, barter, **advertising** of fees.
- **IX Advertising.**

**Psychedelic relevance:** Lifetime ban on sexual intimacy with former clients is among the strictest US therapy rules—closer to MAPS than ACA/APA psychology. **Informed consent** explicitly covers treatments without established standards. **Touch** not named. Couples: who-is-the-client problem if PAT changes one partner’s values (1.8).

## Research ethics (national human-research frameworks)

### US *Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects* (“Common Rule”), 45 CFR 46 — USA — **mandatory** for federally conducted or supported research (and institutions that file an FWA) — 1991; 2018 revisions

**Categories:**

- **Subpart A** — IRBs; assurances; **informed consent** (including 2018 “key information,” broad consent); exemptions; expedited review
- **Subpart B** — pregnant women, fetuses, neonates
- **Subpart C** — **prisoners**
- **Subpart D** — **children**
- FDA parallel at 21 CFR 50/56 for drug trials

**Concepts:** respect for persons, beneficence, justice (Belmont, not itself the Rule); additional protections when autonomy is reduced; **mandatory** IRB; **consent** elements (purpose, risks, benefits, alternatives, confidentiality, compensation for injury, voluntary, COI); deception limits; **vulnerable populations**.

**Psychedelic relevance:** unblinding vs deception; independent consent when dependent; prisoner research almost off-limits for PAT coercion reasons; 2018 “key information” should include identity change, touch, sexual-risk of the setting—not only drug toxicology.

### Canada *TCPS 2* (2022) — **mandatory** for institutions administering Tri-Council funds; de facto national standard — 2022

**Categories — 13 chapters:** 1 Ethics framework (Respect for Persons, Concern for Welfare, Justice); 2 Scope; 3 **Consent** (capacity, documentation, broad consent for biobanks); 4 Fairness and equity in participation; 5 Privacy and **confidentiality**; 6 Governance of REBs; 7 **Conflicts of interest**; 8 Multi-jurisdictional; 9 **Research involving First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Peoples**; 10 Qualitative research; 11 Clinical trials; 12 Human biological materials; 13 Human genetic research.

**Psychedelic relevance:** Chapter 9 is the national research-ethics home for Celidwen-style duties (not identical, but the closest binding Canadian text). Chapter 3 capacity + dependent relationships. Chapter 11 placebo/clinical-trial design vs unblinding.

### Australia NHMRC *National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research* (2025) — **mandatory** for NHMRC-funded work and the reference standard for HRECs (including TGA AP protocols) — 2025

**Categories:**

- Section 1 Values and principles
- Section 2 Risk/benefit and **consent** (including waiver)
- Section 3 Design/conduct (elements of research; biospecimens; genomic; xenotransplantation)
- Section 4 Participants: increased risk; pregnancy/fetus; **children**; **dependent or unequal relationships**; ill-health/disability; other countries; **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people**; disasters; research that may discover illegal activity
- Section 5 Governance: institutions, HRECs, researchers, monitoring, duplication, **COI**, complaints, accountability

**Psychedelic relevance:** TGA AP *requires* HREC approval against this Statement. Chapter 4.4 (unequal relationships) maps onto therapist power in PAT. 4.7 Indigenous research. 4.9 illegal-activity discovery (underground use disclosures).

### NEAC *National Ethical Standards for Health and Disability Research and Quality Improvement* — New Zealand — **national ethical standards** (expected of health research; HDECs apply them) — current

**Categories (Part two, 1–18):** Scope; Ethical principles; **Research and Māori**; Research and Pacific peoples; Disability research; Ethical management of **vulnerability**; **Informed consent**; Benefits and harms; Development and design; Ethical features of studies; Research conduct; Health data; Data and new technologies; Human tissue; Biobanks; Stem cells; Compensation for injury in commercial studies; Quality improvement.

**Psychedelic relevance:** Māori chapter + vulnerability + consent are the PAT research floor; Te Tiriti obligations exceed typical US IRB practice.

### Declaration of Helsinki (WMA, 2024) — international template, not a national statute — **mandatory as adopted by HRECs/IRBs and professional codes (e.g. RANZCP Principle 7)**

Covered in Part A.

## Specialty psychedelic / PAT guidelines (non-national sets)

### MAPS *Code of Ethics for Psychedelic Psychotherapy* (v4) — **recommended** (sponsor/training org; not a licensing board) 

**Categories:** safety; **confidentiality**; transparency; alliance and trust; **touch**; **sexual boundaries** (no sexual contact with participant, former participant, or family—during or after); diversity; non-ordinary states; finances; **competence**; colleagues; relationship to self.

**Criticisms:** Buisson/McNamee/Harrison/Villeneuve — unlicensed co-therapists; complaint routing to PI; “never abandon” vs reported neglect of harmed participants; training that prohibits sex but under-specifies touch-consent timing and bodywork competence; enforcement is internal, not a college.

### HOPE Consensus Statement (2024) — **recommended** scholarly consensus — categories I–X as in Part A.

### Banbury / McGuire *JAMA Network Open* (2024) — **recommended** expert consensus — five issue areas (see A4).

### CRPO (Ontario) professional standards / sexual-abuse regime — **mandatory** for Ontario registered psychotherapists — RHPA + CRPO standards.

Five-year + ongoing power-imbalance sexual rule; no undue influence over wills/POAs; dual relationships only for small-community necessity.

### FSMB (2020) physician sexual misconduct — **recommended** to US medical boards — **de facto binding** when a board adopts it.

Sexual behaviour never diagnostic/therapeutic; includes verbal/virtual; single serious incident may warrant revocation.

### Alberta *Psychedelic Drug Treatment Services Standards* (2023) — **mandatory** in Alberta licensed medical model.

Capacity; full assessment; experimental-nature **consent**; alternatives; COI vs conventional care; therapeutic-**touch** disclosure; continuous monitoring; medical director; abuse-reporting; CDS security; EOL processes.

### College of Alberta Psychologists *PAPT guideline* (30 June 2025) — **mandatory guidance for CAP-regulated psychologists**.

Psychologists do not diagnose need for or recommend specific psychedelic medicines; five years’ independent trauma/mood practice; prep / silent non-directive administration / integration in 1–3 days; unregulated sitters under non-regulated-persons guideline; CPSA recording recommendation; contraindication lists; warn that **WCB, insurers, employers, subpoenas** may compel notes.

### CPSBC ketamine professional guideline (v1.4, 26 August 2026) — **mandatory-style college guideline** for BC physicians.

IV only in hospital or accredited facility; monitor dissociation and post-dose sedation; diversion; off-label unknowns; uninsured-fee fairness; **advertising**; COI; sale-and-dispensing.

### FDA draft psychedelic-trial guidance + Spravato REMS / Janssen Journey — **mandatory** for the approved product; draft guidance **recommended** for sponsors.

Supervised self-administration; enrolled prescriber and pharmacist; no casual take-home.

### Oregon OAR 333-333-5120 / Colorado 4 CCR 755-1 (2024) — **mandatory** for licensed facilitators (adult-use, **not** medical prescribing).

Oregon: client-first; no misrepresentation; default nondirective; emergency recognition; supportive **touch** only with prior written consent; facilitator must not consume product. Colorado: **consent** each session; touch contract; **confidentiality** limits; no dual-authority; sexual/romantic ban **two years** post-termination; no impaired practice; no referral fees; dual-licensees follow the **stricter** code.

### TGA Authorised Prescriber + RANZCP clinical memorandum — **mandatory** pathway in Australia for MDMA/psilocybin.

### Health Canada SAP / trial-risk notices — SAP is **access**, not efficacy endorsement; trial notices: two therapists during dosing; disclose suggestibility, transference, suicidality, cardiovascular risk.

### Ketamine clinician ethics / KAP Code — **recommended** specialty sets (see A7).

### Celidwen eight Rs — **recommended** Indigenous-led ethics; not a state code.

### ACA 2024: **no** adopted ACA psychedelic ethics code; 2014 Code still governs; revision expected 2026.

# Cross-cutting category taxonomy (extracted)

This is the taxonomy of **categories** that actually appear as section headings or principle-titles across the national codes, collapsed into families. Under each family: the **concepts** (duties/rules) that recur, with PAT intensity notes.

1. **Dignity, respect, non-discrimination, cultural safety**  
   Inherent dignity; non-discrimination lists; **cultural competence/safety**; Te Tiriti / TRC / Indigenous chapters; no imposition of practitioner values/religion. *PAT: identity and metaphysics; Celidwen; chaplain non-proselytization.*

2. **Beneficence / non-maleficence / patient welfare first**  
   Consider first the patient’s well-being; do not exploit; risk–benefit; least harm. *PAT: experimental status; ICER/FDA harm; post-trial care gap.*

3. **Autonomy, capacity, informed consent, freedom of consent**  
   Elements of consent; capacity; emergency exception; unestablished-technique extra disclosure; dynamic/enhanced consent; advance directives. *PAT: transformative experience debate; freeze optional permissions after ingestion; non-response.*

4. **Confidentiality, privacy, records, compelled disclosure**  
   Limits; groups/families; recordings; **mandatory reporting**; duty to warn; third-party payers; WCB/subpoena. *PAT: spiritual material as health information; group dosing; military command.*

5. **Competence, scope, innovation, unestablished methods**  
   Boundaries of competence; consultation; change-of-scope; extra disclosure for new techniques; no mandatory personal drug use as credential. *PAT: Track A vs B; CAP five-year rule; RANZCP overall responsibility.*

6. **Loyalty, conflicts, dual/multiple relationships, anti-capture**  
   No business/religious/political exploitation; third-party who-is-the-client; gifts, testimonials, in-person solicitation. *PAT: noetic capture; wills/POAs; clinic volume incentives vs SSRIs/therapy.*

7. **Sexual boundaries**  
   Current-client bans universal; former-client rules **diverge** (APA psychology ~2 years + burden; ACA 5 years; AAMFT never; MAPS never; CRPO 5 years +; Colorado facilitators 2 years). Relatives/partners often included. *PAT: apply the **strictest license in the room**.*

8. **Touch / physical contact**  
   Almost absent from medical/nursing codes except as ordinary care; NASW 1.10 (no psychologically harmful contact); PAT specialty documents make it a **three-tier** doctrine (safety / supportive / sexual).

9. **Abandonment, continuity, interruption, termination, coverage**  
   Reasonable arrangements; delayed-harm window. *PAT: integration and post-trial care; MAPS “never abandon” vs reported gaps.*

10. **Impairment (practitioner and, analogically, patient)**  
    Self-monitoring; colleague reporting; no practice while intoxicated; NAADAC “mood altering chemicals.” *PAT: facilitator must not dose; ABA 1.14 analog for the **patient’s** NOSC.*

11. **Honesty, advertising, fees, records as property**  
    No false claims; testimonial limits; fair uninsured fees; barter caution; CDS accountability. *PAT: hype, guru narratives, suggestible-window testimonials.*

12. **Research, innovation, evidence, equipoise**  
    Helsinki/TCPS2/Common Rule/NHMRC/NEAC; COI; placebo; vulnerable groups; Indigenous research. *PAT: unblinding, sponsor training, independent harms research.*

13. **Safety, environment, collaboration, most-responsible clinician**  
    Teamwork; reporting unsafe systems; nursing independent duty. *PAT: three-role ketamine split; two-person default; no virtual-only IV.*

14. **Justice, access, public health, stewardship, environment**  
    Equitable access; resource stewardship; global/environmental health (ANA 10); corporate determinants (CPHA). *PAT: who is in trials; retreat classism; ecosystem harvest.*

15. **Accountability, self-regulation, complaints, mandatory reporting**  
    Ethics committees vs **licensing colleges**; candour; external reporting (FDA PDAC). *PAT: complaint path that leaves the building.*

16. **Education, supervision, students**  
    No sexual with supervisees; accurate program description; mandatory therapy of trainees (APA 7.05 caution). *PAT: sponsor-only training criticised.*

17. **Technology, distance practice, social media, recording**  
    ACA H; AAMFT VI; NAADAC encryption. *PAT: recording as abuse-deterrent vs marketing asset; virtual prep ≠ dosing accountability.*

18. **Special populations and dependent relationships**  
    Children, prisoners, pregnant people, diminished capacity, unequal relationships (NHMRC 4.4; Helsinki independent consent-taker). *PAT: adolescents exceptional; carceral; MAID; dementia (Ixtlan).*

# Criticisms of the codes (commentators)

- **Association vs regulator:** AMA, APA, ACA, NASW, AAMFT, NAADAC, APC bind *members*; patients need **college/board** remedies. Dual-licensees (Colorado rule) should follow the stricter instrument.
- **Non-ranking of principles:** NASW explicitly refuses to rank; CPA *does* rank. Critics say unranked codes become litigation tools without moral guidance (Reamer; 2025 *J. Social Work Values & Ethics* paper).
- **Risk-management / liability tone:** psychology and social-work codes accused of protecting institutions over justice (O’Donohue 2019; Teo 2015 “morally oblique”).
- **Enforcement theatre:** Hoffman Report (APA); MAPS internal ethics vs unlicensed abusers; complaint routing to PIs.
- **Silence on touch, NOSC, identity, suggestibility:** national medical/nursing/pharmacy codes do not see PAT. Therapy codes see dual relationships and sex but not drug-amplified suggestibility.
- **Divergent sexual clocks:** 0 vs 2 vs 5 years vs lifetime — PAT commentators argue for the lifetime/MAPS/AAMFT/CRPO pole, not Colorado’s two-year facilitator clock.
- **Cultural safety uneven:** strongest in NZ/AU/Canadian nursing and CPA/TCPS2 ch. 9; weakest in older US medical/pharmacy texts (CMA 2026 and ANA 2025 catching up).
- **Exceptionalism trap:** rewriting a “psychedelic code” that *lowers* evidentiary bars (AJOB 2024–25) vs the opposite trap of declaring consent impossible.
- **Spiritual-care gap:** APC non-imposition is right; medical PAT teams rarely import it.
- **Research-to-practice translation:** Brittain 2025 — Helsinki-grade trial ethics do not automatically become clinic ethics (post-trial care, competency, business models).
